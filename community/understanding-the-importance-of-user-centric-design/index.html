Understanding the Importance of User-Centric Design

User-centric design starts from the people who will use the software, and checks the result against what they need. This lesson sets out why it comes first on every job, and where it shows in the work.

1. Meeting User Needs

User-centric design starts with putting the needs, desires, and preferences of the people who use the software at the forefront of the development process. That way, the software you create answers the problems and challenges users face every day, and can be checked against them.

2. Improved User Experience

A user-centric approach results in a better user experience. A customer finds the hours, the menu or the booking button where they look for it, on the phone in their hand, and comes back because it worked the first time.

3. Reduced Friction and Frustration

When software is designed with users in mind, it minimizes friction and reduces frustration. Users encounter fewer obstacles and challenges while using the software, leading to a more productive and enjoyable experience.

4. Increased Adoption Rates

Staff keep using a tool that fits how they already work, and quietly go back to paper when it doesn’t. Software nobody uses has cost its full price and returned nothing, so adoption is part of the result.

5. Competitive Advantage

Where customers have a choice, user-centric design can set your product apart. Software that stands out for its user-friendliness and effectiveness is more likely to gain a competitive edge and attract more users.

6. Lower Support and Maintenance Costs

Well-designed software that meets user needs from the start is less likely to require extensive support and maintenance. This translates to cost savings in the long run. On the code side, the lecture Functional Programming Maintenance Strategy covers what keeps software easy to maintain: small functions, strict types, tests and documentation.

7. User Feedback and Iteration

A user-centric approach encourages the collection of user feedback and enables iterative improvements. This means that software can evolve and adapt to changing user needs over time.

8. Building Trust and Credibility

Software that does what it said, every time, earns the trust of the people who use it: the cook believes the sold-out list, and the customer believes the hours.

9. Reduced Development Risks

Understanding user needs and preferences reduces the risk of developing software that doesn’t resonate with the target audience. This, in turn, minimizes the risk of project failure or underutilization.

10. Fulfilling the Purpose of Software

Ultimately, the purpose of software is to serve users and help them accomplish tasks efficiently. A user-centric design checks the software against that purpose, story by story, before launch.

Where it bends. The person who pays is rarely the person who uses the result. The owner signs the written scope; a customer on a phone, two streets away, uses the page. User-centric design speaks for that customer, and when the two pull apart, say the owner wants the shop’s history at the top of the page while most visitors came for the hours, the webmaster makes the customer’s case and the scope records what the owner decides, as a line such as “the hours show without scrolling on a phone”. Some users never reach an interview at all: a customer who uses a screen reader, one who zooms the text to twice its size, one who uses only a keyboard. Their needs are already written down, in WCAG 2.1 AA, so a design is checked against it rather than waiting for one of them to complain. And user-centric never means doing whatever a user asks: Identifying User Needs shows a request that could be met perfectly while the need behind it went unmet.

In summary, user-centric design is the principle the rest of the course checks against: the stories, the scenarios and the tests all start from what a user needs. The next lesson, Defining User Stories, sets out what a story contains.
