KFalls AI Meetup (evening, business & student)

An advanced SI protocol ecosystem

An agreement wherever two systems meet, behavior laid out as points we can measure, and code that checks the model’s work and has the last word

Welcome, everyone, and thank you for coming. This one goes deep, and we’ll keep it plain enough for a first look. A few made-up friends keep us company along the way: a coffee cart, a bakery and a salon. And when we say the model, we mean the trained part of the system, the part that reads and writes words. If you run a business, listen for the places where things get checked before a customer ever sees them. If you’re a student, listen for the agreements, the contracts; writing those well is the craft. And if you’re an engineer, do catch us if a word gets loose.

Where things break: the joins

A booking form sends an appointment to a calendar. A little program reads the price list each morning. An assistant places an order with a coffee cart.

Each one is a join, a place where something crosses from one side to the other. When nothing checks what crosses, it’s the customer who finds the mistake first.

Let’s try this on a business you know, maybe your own. Where does something cross from one side to the other: a form, a price list, a booking? And what checks it today? If you can’t name anything, that’s the join to look after first, and in a few minutes we’ll see how.

SI: what federal agencies now call AI

Since September 29, 2026, Executive Order 14434 directs federal agencies to write Super Intelligence, SI, where they used to write artificial intelligence, AI. It covers their own documents and communications.

The order changes the name, not the law: SI means what AI’s definition in federal law already covers, so the two words name the same systems. Proposed wording for a federal definition is due to the President within 60 days.

In research, “superintelligence” means something else. Here, SI is only the new name.

Sources: the order and its fact sheet

We’ll say this once, plainly, and then move on. The order is titled “Inaugurating the Era of Super Intelligence.” It applies to the executive branch’s departments and agencies from now on; the laws Congress has passed, regulations and earlier documents stay just as they are. For its purposes, SI means what the law’s definition of AI covers. For anyone who likes to look things up, that’s title 15 of the U.S. Code, section 9401, paragraph 3. When researchers say superintelligence, they usually mean a hypothetical system that would far outdo people at nearly everything, and that’s a different idea. The new name makes no claim about what these systems can do. From here on, we’ll say SI too.

One join, one agreement

Each join an SI system crosses has an open agreement of its own, often a protocol: a contract anyone can read and build to.

MCP joins an app to its tools. A2A joins one agent to another. OpenAPI describes a web service, an API, to the programs that call it.

When a new protocol comes along, ask it three things: which two things does it join, what crosses, and what checks it?

Let’s unpack a few names. An agent, a bit like a travel agent, is an SI system that takes steps on someone’s behalf, like the assistant ordering from the coffee cart. MCP is the Model Context Protocol. Its revision of July 28, 2026 dropped the opening handshake, the hello in which the two sides used to trade versions and say what each could do, so don’t be surprised if an older tutorial still teaches it. A2A is the Agent2Agent protocol, now at version 1.0. AGENTS.md and Agent Skills are open agreements too, but they’re file formats an agent reads, not protocols that run over a connection. And llms.txt is still a proposal.

Who decides

A server, the program on the other side of the join, offers three kinds of things. MCP sorts them by one question: who decides when each one gets used?

The model calls tools. The app attaches resources. A person picks prompts.

Even so, a person should always be able to say no when the model reaches for a tool.

When we say the model decides, we never mean nobody’s watching. Let’s sort a few together: today’s menu is a resource, adding an item is a tool, and a template for the week’s special, a ready-made starting point a person picks from a list, is a prompt. And a refund? That’s a tool too, one a person approves every single time it’s called.

Which “geometric reasoning” do we mean?

  • A declared design space: we choose in advance the ways a behavior can vary, and each behavior becomes a point. This is the one we mean.
  • The shape inside a model: the numbers it works with, its representations, with reasoning pictured as a path through them.
  • Geometry itself: a model solving geometry problems.
  • Geometric deep learning, a neighboring field: models built for data with a shape, such as a photo’s grid of pixels or a map of roads.

You’ll hear this phrase used at least three ways, and a neighboring field shares half of it. So when it comes up, it’s kind to say which one you mean before anyone starts arguing. The rest of this talk builds on the first one.

Points, traits and distance

The cart’s answers about its menu can vary in three ways, called traits: brief or detailed, formal or casual, cautious or bold.

A point picks one side of each, written 0 for the first and 1 for the second, so 011 is brief, casual and bold. Three traits, two sides each: eight points.

Distance is how many traits differ: 011 and 111 are one apart.

Think of three little switches for how the cart answers, each one either up or down. The key under the cube calls the traits by short names: length for brief or detailed, formality for formal or casual, and certainty for cautious or bold. Each trait is one axis of the space, one direction you can move in. If you’ve met these ideas before, a statistician would call this a two-level full factorial, and a coder would call the distance Hamming distance. We don’t keep a separate list of the points or the distances; we work them out from the three traits every time, so they can never drift out of step. The table beside the cube holds a made-up number for each point, standing in for whatever we’d measure there. And for the owners here: your assistant’s voice is something you choose on purpose. It’s one point in the design, and that’s where we measure it.

Change one fact, and the answer must flip

A pair is two fact sheets in made-up words, one fact apart.

Flip a fact on the violet path, the proof, and the answer must move. Flip a side fact, a distractor, and it must not.

So we score pairs, not single questions. Always saying yes gets every yes question right, but no pair that should flip.

A guess that ignores the flip gets no pair right.

Let’s read the violet path from the top: the brintepet is a lomkellet, every lomkellet is a kellbrinet, and every kellbrinet is dairy-free, so the answer is yes. The changed sheet says instead that no kellbrinet is dairy-free, and the answer turns to no. That’s what the little “was” and “now” on the last arrow mean. Why made-up words? If we said oat milk, a model could answer from what it already knows about oat milk, without reading the facts in front of it. Made-up words mean it has to read them. Researchers build pairs like these by hand and call them contrast sets, and you may hear the two sheets called canonical, the original, and perturbed, the changed one. Here one program writes the pairs, and another, which we call the oracle, follows the facts to the right answer for both sheets, so nobody types an answer by hand.

Hold the facts, move the voice

Take one fact sheet and answer it once at each of the eight points: that’s a matched family.

The facts and the answer stay locked; only the voice moves. So when two answers differ, we can trace the difference to the traits that differ.

For the cart, the tone can change and the oat milk answer can’t.

It’s a bit like one recipe served eight ways: the ingredients never change, only the plating. Every part of an answer, every field, is marked one of two ways: invariant, meaning it stays locked, or expressive, meaning it may change. If nobody marked a part, we count that as a mistake, rather than quietly guessing which kind it is. And the checker lists every mistake at once, so one round of fixes can clear them all.

The model proposes; typed code decides

A harness is the plain, predictable program wrapped around a model. The two work in tandem, side by side, on every request.

For a salon booking, the model proposes a time and a reply. Typed code decides which times are offered, which reply goes out and which time is held for the customer.

One list of open times feeds both the model and the checker.

Think of a harness on a horse: the horse brings the strength, and the harness guides where it goes. By typed code we mean code in which every value has a declared kind, a time, say, or a booking, and the computer checks those kinds before the program ever runs. Researchers have a name for this pairing, LLM-Modulo: they argue that a language model can’t verify its own plans, so they give it checkers outside the model. Here one offer list feeds two things: the schema, a form that limits what the model can write, and the checker, which decides what the harness accepts. And a property test tries many cases the computer makes up, to be sure those two keep agreeing. On the drawing, a turn is one message from the customer and the reply to it.

Every failure is refused, never passed through

  • A reply goes out exactly as written, or not at all.
  • A rejected answer goes back to the model with every reason it was turned down, for another try, until one passes or the tries run out.
  • Out of tries or out of time, the customer hears the salon’s own line, and the failure stays in the records.

Think of a shop door that stays locked when the power goes out, rather than swinging open. Engineers call this failing closed: when something goes wrong, the model’s words don’t go out, and the salon’s own line does. The fallback has no place in it for a reply or a held time, so it can’t carry one by accident. What went wrong inside stays in the records, away from the customer, and every reply that did go out can be traced back through the records to what made it.

The app and the server, in tandem

A booking takes two rounds, and the server remembers nothing about it in between.

The server asks which time, and the app asks the customer. Round two sends the booking again, with the answer and a signed note of what was offered. The note works only once.

The customer answers one question, and a time the server never offered can’t be booked.

Two rounds like these have a name in MCP: a multi round-trip request. The app is MCP’s client here, and the signed note is what MCP calls the request state. Think of a stamped coat-check ticket: the server can tell if anyone has changed it, it’s good for only a short while, and it works only once. A time that was never offered is turned away before anything is booked. And the app is just as careful, the way you’d check a delivery against the order slip: it reads every reply against the agreed shape, and either every part it uses checks out, or it uses none of it.

Extend, don’t fork

A harness grows at the places made for add-ons; the model and the core loop stay fixed.

One question sorts every addition: is it a judgment call, or must it happen every time?

Judgment calls go where the model can read them. What must happen every time goes into code.

To fork is to copy the core and change your copy, and that’s what we steer away from. An instruction, in a skill or a standing instructions file, is a request; the model may not follow it every time. A hook is a small piece of code that runs at a set moment: before a tool call, after one or at the end. It runs every time, and one that runs before a call can stop the call outright, so it’s a rule, not a request. An instruction is a note on the fridge; a hook is the lock on the till. A subagent is a helper the model hands one job to, and it starts fresh, in a context of its own. It’s the same with a harness built into an app: it grows by adding parts, never by changing its core, and every addition comes with a scenario that tests it, a short written example of what it must do. And for the owners here: an add-on comes along by itself at the next update, while a patched core has to be patched again by hand.

Questions owners ask

Is this hype?

Fair question. We give each claim a row in a ledger: measured, released for anyone to check, or only asserted. A maker’s promise that its assistant never double-books stays asserted until a test calendar checks it.

Things change too fast.

They do change quickly. Protocols get new versions, so it helps to write down which version you built on. The joins themselves hold still, and so do the checks at each one.

A claims ledger is just a list: each claim, where it came from, how far it’s been shown, and what would show it false. Measured means someone tested it and showed how; released means its code and data are out for anyone to check; asserted means someone only said so. For this promise, the source is the maker’s own page and the status is asserted, and two overlapping bookings in a test calendar would show it false. So we build on what’s been measured or released, and an assertion waits in the ledger until something checks it.

Questions students ask

SI builds websites for free.

Drafts did get cheap. Deciding what may cross a join, checking it and standing behind it didn’t.

SI will replace developers.

The model proposes. Someone still writes the contracts, the checks and the scenarios that decide. That’s the engineering, and it’s yours to learn.

We won’t try to tell the future here. Part of the work really did get cheap, and it’s honest to say so. The other part, standing behind the result, still takes a person, and that’s the craft worth learning.

What we covered, and your questions

  • Every join gets an open agreement, and we ask each one: which two things does it join, what crosses, and what checks it?
  • Geometric reasoning, as we mean it: behaviors laid out in advance as points, and measured in pairs.
  • In a tandem harness, the model proposes and typed code decides, and both work from one list.
  • Every failure is refused, never passed through.
  • A harness grows at the places made for add-ons; the core loop stays fixed.

Thank you for staying with us through all of that. Now it’s your turn: what would you like to ask? And here’s something small to take home. If you run a business, pick one join it runs on, a form, a price list or a booking, and ask what checks it today. If you’re a student or an engineer, try the same on any app you use or build: which two things does it join, what crosses, and what checks it? Wherever the answer is nothing, that’s the join to give an agreement first.
